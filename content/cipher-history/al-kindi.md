story: |
  In the 9th century the philosopher al-Kindi, who worked for the caliphs, wrote a short treatise on reading secret messages. His idea: in every language some letters are common and others are rare. So count the letters of a long ordinary text, then count the signs of the secret message, and match them up: the most common sign with the most common letter, the second with the second, and so on. It is the oldest description of this method that survives. A copy was found in a library in Istanbul and printed in 1987.
more: |
  Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi was born in Basra and educated in Baghdad, and he served at the court of the caliphs. Later writers called him "the philosopher of the Arabs". He wrote on philosophy, mathematics, medicine and much more; the treatise on secret writing is one short work among many.

  For his counts he took a sample of ordinary Arabic text of 3,667 letters, and wrote down which letter came first, second, third and so on. Then, he explains, you do the same with the signs of the secret message and give each sign the letter of the same rank.

  The method has a catch that you will meet in "Your turn": in a short text the ranks do not come out exactly right. Counting gives you a start. Common words, and letters that often stand together, give you the rest.

truth: |
  Al-Kindi's *Risala fi istikhraj al-mu'amma*, a treatise on cryptanalysis, is the oldest known text that explains how to break a substitution cipher by counting letters. The manuscript was found in the Süleymaniye Library in Istanbul by three researchers from Damascus, Mohammed Mrayati, Yahya Meer Alam and Hassan at-Tayyan, who edited it and printed it in Arabic in 1987. Their English translation, *al-Kindi's Treatise on Cryptanalysis*, is the first book of the series *The Arabic Origins of Cryptology*.

  Later Arab writers on secret writing, such as ibn Dunaynir and ibn Adlan in the 13th century, built on his method.
