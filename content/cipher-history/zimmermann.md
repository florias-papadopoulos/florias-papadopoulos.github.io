story: |
  On 16 January 1917 the German foreign minister, Arthur Zimmermann, sent a telegram in code to his ambassador in Washington, to be passed on to Mexico. Germany was about to start unrestricted submarine warfare on 1 February. If that brought the United States into the war, Mexico was to be offered an alliance, money, and the chance to win back Texas, New Mexico and Arizona. Britain had cut Germany's own Atlantic cables at the start of the war, so the telegram went over other countries' lines, including American diplomatic cables that passed through Britain. There the codebreakers of the British Admiralty, known as Room 40, read it.
quote:
  text: "We intend to begin on the first of February unrestricted submarine warfare. We shall endeavor in spite of this to keep the United States of America neutral. In the event of this not succeeding, we make Mexico a proposal or alliance on the following basis: make war together, make peace together, generous financial support and an understanding on our part that Mexico is to reconquer the lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona."
  source: "The decoded telegram as the State Department received it, National Archives (USA), Record Group 59, ID 302024 (public domain)."
more: |
  Room 40 was run by William Reginald Hall, the Director of Naval Intelligence. The team that read the telegram included Nigel de Grey; his own account of the work, written in 1945, is in The National Archives.

  Hall had a problem. Showing the telegram to the Americans would tell them that Britain read their diplomatic cables, and would tell Germany that its code was broken. So he had a copy obtained in Mexico City, from the telegraph office there: the version that the German embassy in Washington had forwarded to Mexico, in another code, numbered 13040. Now the story could be that it had been found in Mexico.

  On 24 February the American ambassador in London, Walter Page, cabled the text to Washington. It was in American newspapers on 1 March. Many said it was a British forgery, until Zimmermann himself admitted that it was genuine. The United States declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917.

truth: |
  **The code.** German diplomats used codebooks of numbered words and phrases. A codebook can be rebuilt without ever capturing it: from many intercepted messages, a guessed word here, a group that keeps coming back there, and the context. Room 40 read the telegram, and the copy from Mexico, in code 13040, let Britain pass the text to the Americans without revealing where it really came from. The National Archives in London still hold that codebook.

  **What followed.** Wilson asked Congress for war on 2 April 1917. The historian David Kahn wrote of the telegram: "No other single cryptanalysis has had such enormous consequences."

  **About the puzzle.** The real telegram was in German, in German codes. Our codebook and its numbers are made up; the words are those of the State Department's English decode, with one slip ("or" for "of") put right.
