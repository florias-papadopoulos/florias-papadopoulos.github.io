story: |
  In 1586 Mary, Queen of Scots, had been a prisoner in England for eighteen years. Anthony Babington and his friends plotted to kill Queen Elizabeth and put Mary on the throne, and Mary and Babington wrote to each other in a code of signs, smuggled in beer barrels. But the channel was run by Elizabeth's spymaster, Francis Walsingham: every letter was opened and read by his codebreaker, Thomas Phelippes, then sealed again and sent on. When Mary's answer of 17 July approved the plan, Phelippes drew a gallows on it. Before it reached Babington, a forged postscript was added in the same code, asking for the names of the six men who were to kill the queen.

more: |
  Mary was held at Chartley under a strict keeper, Sir Amias Paulet, who let almost no letter through. The way in and out was the beer: a brewer delivered it in barrels and took the empty ones away, and the letters travelled hidden inside. The courier, Gilbert Gifford, was a double agent working for Walsingham.

  Mary's letters were put into cipher by her secretary Gilbert Curll. The key between her and Babington survives: after his arrest Babington signed it, adding that "this last is the alphabet by which only I have written unto the Queen of Scots or received letters from her".

truth: |
  **The cipher.** Mary used nomenclators like this one: a substitution alphabet with extra signs for common words and names. The National Archives still hold several of her cipher keys. A nomenclator is only as safe as the person who carries it, and here the carrier worked for the other side.

  **The end.** Walsingham ordered Babington's arrest; he escaped for a few days, was caught, and was executed with his friends. Mary's secretaries were questioned and confessed that they had written the letter of 17 July at her command. Mary was tried in 1586 and executed on 8 February 1587.

  **About the puzzle.** The signs follow the key that Babington signed (The National Archives, SP 12/193/54), drawn with characters your browser can show; the word signs are simplified, since the real key has signs for many more words. The text is section vii of the letter, spelling modernised. Mary's own letter does not survive: Babington burned it, and we know it from contemporary copies, the closest being The National Archives, SP 53/18/53.
disagree:
  - "**Who wrote the postscript?** A draft of it survives in Phelippes's handwriting, and Kahn (1967) calls the forgery and its encipherment Phelippes's work. The British Library says Walsingham had it added. Pollen (1922) points out that Walsingham owned up to it: on 3 August he wrote to Phelippes that he feared \"the addytyon of the postscrypt\" had made Babington suspicious."
  - "**Was more than the postscript changed?** The historian William Camden wrote that Phelippes added the postscript \"if not some other things also\", and the British Library says several passages were amended as well. Pollen, who compared the copies, concluded that the postscript was the only forgery."
  - "**How hard was the codebreaking?** Kahn says Phelippes solved the letters almost as fast as they arrived. Pollen, who re-deciphered many of Phelippes's papers, wrote that the work was easy in 1586: the ciphers had just been changed, and Phelippes copied the new keys as they passed through Walsingham's hands."
