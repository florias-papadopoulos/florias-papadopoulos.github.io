story: |
  In the online archive of the Bibliothèque nationale de France, three codebreakers came across pages written entirely in signs. The catalogue said they concerned Italian matters of the early 16th century. George Lasry, a computer scientist, Norbert Biermann, a pianist and music professor, and Satoshi Tomokiyo, a physicist, broke the cipher with a computer and then by hand. The language was not Italian but French, the writer was a woman in captivity, and the name Walsingham kept coming up. They were letters of Mary, Queen of Scots, written while she was a prisoner in England, most of them to the French ambassador in London.
more: |
  The letters date from 1578 to 1584, and together they hold about 50,000 words. Mary wrote most of them to Michel de Castelnau de Mauvissière, the French ambassador to England. She complains about her health and the conditions of her captivity, and writes about her talks with Elizabeth I about her release.

  The paper appeared in the journal *Cryptologia* in February 2023. It quotes the historian John Bossy, who had written that Mary's secret correspondence before mid 1583 was "kept so secure that none of it has survived, and we don't know what was in it". The new letters are most likely part of it. The historian John Guy called the discovery "the most important new find on Mary, Queen of Scots for 100 years".

  To be sure, the team compared them with plain copies of a few of the same letters among Walsingham's papers in English archives. They match.

truth: |
  **The cipher.** In Mary's key a common letter had several signs, and there were extra signs for frequent words and for names. That defeats plain letter counting. It does not defeat a patient search: the team used **hill climbing**, a method that starts from a random key, changes it a little at a time and keeps each change that makes the text read better. The computer got them part of the way; they found the rest by hand, sign by sign.

  **Why it worked.** A homophonic cipher hides how often each letter appears, but not which letters follow which. Pairs and runs of letters like "th", "ion" or "the" still have to come out right, and a computer can measure that for a huge number of trial keys.

  **The letters and Babington.** These letters end in 1584. Two years later Mary wrote to Anthony Babington in another of her ciphers, and that letter cost her life: see [the Babington plot](/cipher-history/babington/).
